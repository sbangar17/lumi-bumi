The quick answer

You mentioned glass cubes, but I don't know whether yours are hollow or solid. That decides most of the choice:

Your cubeBest optionWhy

You want a "prism" look or a low flat baseC — right-angle prismPicture appears on a vertical glass face; extra firmware flip needed

My recommendation: do A or B first, depending on your cube. It's the fastest way to a working gift. Make sure you build and test everything outside the glass first, then fit it. Option D is a good second version if you want it tougher.

For a child: glass edges can be sharp and glass breaks. If the cube will be handled a lot, consider acrylic or polycarbonate cubes (same look, much safer), sand or tape any glass edge, and keep the whole stack out of reach of small fingers.

Know your parts first

The screen's lit area is much smaller than the board: about 22 × 11 mm. That is the size of the picture in any housing without a magnifier. The cube needs a minimum interior of roughly 30 × 30 × 20 mm for the whole stack.

A few facts about light and glass

  • Flat glass doesn't magnify. A cube or a flat window shows the picture at its real size. Only curved glass (a dome, sphere or lens) enlarges it.
  • Glass makes things appear closer. Looking straight down through glass of index about 1.5, a picture seems about two-thirds as deep as it really is. That's why option B looks like the image floats inside.
  • Avoid air gaps. Between the screen and the glass, air causes reflections and sometimes rainbow rings. A thin layer of clear silicone, optical gel or clear tape removes them and makes the picture brighter.
  • Black surroundings improve contrast. A black bezel, black card or dark paint around the window hides the screen's edges and stops glare.
  • The OLED has a wide viewing angle (it looks fine from the side), so tilting or side views work.
  • Prisms and total internal reflection. In a right-angle prism the sloped face reflects light fully, but only while it's clean and touching air. A drop of glue, a fingerprint or tape on that face breaks the effect.

A · Hollow glass cube (screen on the inside of the front glass)

Easy$Looks: glass cube with a glowing window

How it works

The OLED's screen is stuck to the inside of one glass face with a thin layer of clear tape or silicone, so the picture shines straight through. The ESP32, buzzer and buttons sit on a small base plate behind and below it.

You need

  • A hollow glass cube (display box, terrarium or jewellery cube) with an opening or lid
  • A base plate cut to fit: thin wood, 3 mm acrylic or foam board
  • Clear double-sided tape or a little clear silicone
  • Foam tape or 2 mm standoffs
  • Small cable notch or hole at the back

Build steps

  1. Measure the inside of the cube. The stack needs about 30 × 30 × 20 mm minimum.
  2. Cut the base plate and drill two button holes at the front edge and a notch for the cable.
  3. Stick the OLED to the glass with clear tape or silicone. Check there are no bubbles.
  4. Fix the ESP32 and buzzer to the plate with foam tape. Keep the USB-C port facing the cable notch.
  5. Dry-fit, power up, test all messages, then fix the plate permanently.

👍 Good

  • Simplest build and the screen is very bright and sharp
  • Easy to open and service if the top lifts off
  • Looks like a premium display piece

⚠️ Watch out

  • Sealed glass muffles the buzzer. Leave a gap or hole, or put the buzzer on a non-glass face
  • Glass edges: sand or tape them if a child will handle it
  • If the screen is too far from the glass you see reflections

B · Solid glass or crystal cube with the screen underneath

Easy$Looks: the picture floats inside the glass

How it works

If your cube is solid K9 crystal, nothing can go inside, so the screen goes under it. Light enters the glass and you see the picture through the top. Because glass bends light, a picture seen through thick glass looks about one-third closer than it really is, so it appears to sit inside the cube.

You need

  • The solid cube (a flat bottom face is essential)
  • A base block: wood, 3D-printed, or a small box
  • Clear silicone sheet, optical gel or optically clear glue (no air gap)
  • A black recess or bezel around the screen to stop stray light

Build steps

  1. Mark where the screen will sit on the cube's bottom face. Make sure the centre is where you want the picture.
  2. Make the base with a shallow pocket for the OLED, screen facing up, and room below for the ESP32 and buzzer.
  3. Put a thin layer of clear silicone or gel on the OLED, then lower the cube onto it. Press gently and let it cure level.
  4. Put the buttons on the side of the base so your hand doesn't block the view.
  5. Test from several angles. Adjust the contrast in software if it looks washed out.

👍 Good

  • Works with any solid cube. No cutting glass
  • The picture looks like it floats in the crystal
  • Very tidy: no visible parts around the cube

⚠️ Watch out

  • Flat glass does not magnify. The picture stays 22 × 11 mm, so it's small in a big cube
  • An air gap causes glare and rainbow rings. Couple it to the glass
  • Engraved 3D patterns inside the crystal can show through the picture
Make it bigger: use a smaller cube (40–50 mm) so the picture fills more of it, or add a magnifier (option E or a flat Fresnel lens) between the screen and the glass.

C · Right-angle prism periscope (the prism option)

Medium$$Looks: picture on a vertical glass face while the screen lies flat

How it works

A right-angle prism (like the ones in binoculars) sits on top of the OLED. Light from the screen goes up, bounces off the sloped face by total internal reflection and comes out of the upright face towards you. The screen stays flat, so the build is low and the board is easy to fit.

You need

  • A right-angle glass prism, leg length about 25 mm (it must cover the 22 mm wide screen area). Search for “25 mm right angle prism BK7”
  • A base that holds the OLED flat, screen up
  • Clear silicone only between the OLED and the prism's bottom face
  • Black card or paint around the screen to cut stray light

Build steps

  1. Place the prism on the OLED with the sloped face pointing away from you and the upright face towards you.
  2. Attach the bottom face to the screen with a thin layer of clear silicone. Never put glue, tape or fingerprints on the sloped face. Total internal reflection needs clean glass touching air.
  3. Power up. The picture will be mirrored or upside down, which is normal. Rotating or flipping it in software fixes it.
  4. Build a small hood or frame so only the upright face shows.
  5. Mount the buttons on the front of the base.

👍 Good

  • A genuinely different look: the screen disappears and the picture is on a crystal face
  • The housing can be very low and neat
  • The prism also keeps dust off the screen

⚠️ Watch out

  • Mirrored image: needs a display flip option in the firmware (I can add one)
  • A little dimmer and a little smaller than direct viewing
  • Prisms cost more than plain glass, and the sizes must match your screen
  • Any finger marks or glue on the sloped face spoil it
Why 25 mm? The active screen area is about 22 × 11 mm. A prism with legs under about 22 mm would crop the picture. Measure your own screen first, since module sizes vary.

D · 3D-printed (or laser-cut) housing with a window

Medium$ – $$Looks: modern, fully custom

How it works

A box sized to your parts (or to fit snugly inside a glass cube) holds the electronics and gives you precise button holes and a cable slot. Use a dark or smoked front so the screen's edges are hidden, and mount the OLED on short standoffs behind a window.

You need

  • Access to a 3D printer (or a print service / library / maker space), or a laser cutter for acrylic
  • Black or dark grey filament, plus smoked or frosted acrylic for a window
  • M2 screws and 2 mm standoffs for the OLED (the board has 4 corner holes)
  • Your measured part sizes

Build steps

  1. Measure your OLED, ESP32, buzzer and buttons. The part sizes diagram below is a starting guide.
  2. Model: a 4–5 mm wall box with a front window about 24 × 13 mm, two button holes, a USB-C slot and a few small sound holes.
  3. Print a test piece first. Check the screen fits and the buttons click.
  4. Assemble with standoffs and foam tape. Close with 4 screws or snap fit.
  5. Optional: put the printed cube inside the glass cube so the glass is a cover.

👍 Good

  • Exact fit and a professional finish
  • Hides wiring and protects the electronics
  • Safest for children: no loose parts and no sharp edges
  • Easy to make two identical cubes

⚠️ Watch out

  • Needs a printer or print service and a bit of design time
  • The window must match the screen carefully, or you see the module edges
No 3D printer? A small project box, a pencil-case-sized wooden box, or even a sturdy empty tin with a hand-cut window can work as a first version.

E · Glass dome (cloche) over a tilted screen

Easy$ – $$Looks: a keepsake under glass

How it works

A wooden base holds the screen on a wedge, tilted towards you like a tiny desk clock, and a glass or acrylic dome covers everything. A thick curved dome adds a slight magnifying effect.

You need

  • A glass or acrylic dome with a matching wooden or acrylic base
  • A small 3D-printed or wooden wedge (about 30° tilt)
  • Foam tape, short wires
  • A hole in the base for the cable

Build steps

  1. Cut or print the wedge so the OLED tilts about 30° back.
  2. Hide the ESP32 and buzzer in a pocket in the base.
  3. Put the buttons on the front edge of the base, outside the dome.
  4. Seat the dome on the base with a felt ring.

👍 Good

  • Pretty, and keeps dust and fingers off the screen
  • Buttons stay outside, so nothing needs to open

⚠️ Watch out

  • Domes distort the picture a little at the edges
  • Sound is muffled; add holes in the base
  • Glass domes are fragile: use acrylic for a child

F · Floating picture pyramid (experimental)

Medium$Looks: ghostly picture hovering in a pyramid

How it works

This is the “hologram pyramid” effect (Pepper's ghost). A clear four-sided pyramid placed on the screen reflects the picture so it seems to float in the middle when you view it from the side.

You need

  • A small clear acrylic pyramid, about 25–30 mm
  • A dark room and the OLED at full contrast

Build steps

  1. Lay the OLED screen up on the base.
  2. Stand the pyramid point-up on the screen.
  3. View from the side in a dark room.

👍 Good

  • Fun, cheap, and quick to try

⚠️ Watch out

  • Only works in the dark and the picture is tiny and dim
  • Only one of the four faces shows the picture clearly
  • Not suitable as the main design for a gift. Try it as a bonus

Make it cute for your niece 🎀

The electronics are the same whichever housing you pick. What makes it a gift is the character. Here are ideas, from easy to ambitious:

Give them personalities

  • Two characters. Lumi is warm yellow, Bumi is pink (or her favourite colour). Paint, felt or washi tape on the base in each colour.
  • Ears and feet. Cat, bear or bunny ears cut from felt or foam and glued to the lid or the base. Add felt feet so it doesn't scratch furniture.
  • A face on the case. Stickers for cheeks and a mouth below the screen make the screen look like the character's eyes or forehead. Blush circles are cheap and effective.
  • Name labels. Her name on Bumi, yours on Lumi, in a rounded font on a vinyl sticker, or use a label maker.

Make the buttons friendly

  • Colour-code them: A is a sunny yellow cap for "Hi" and "Miss you", B is a pink cap for "Love you" and "Good night".
  • Tiny icons next to each: a smiley beside A and a heart beside B, drawn with a paint pen or a sticker.
  • Big caps: add soft silicone or round button caps (or even a pompom on a short stalk) so little fingers find them.
  • Different feel: make A and B different heights or textures so she can tell them apart in the dark.

Cute touches on the screen (software)

  • Her own pictures. Add her favourite animal, a unicorn or a star as extra quick-message icons. I can draw them in the same 8 × 8 pixel style.
  • An animated face. The idle screen can blink or wink instead of just showing the name.
  • Greeting by name: "Hi [her name]!" when the cube wakes up.
  • Tunes for each message: a lullaby for Good night, a happy jingle for Hi.
  • A gentle night mode: dim the screen and mute sounds after bedtime.

Make it magical

  • A soft glow. A small warm LED or a ring of coloured LEDs under the cube that pulses when a message arrives (this needs one extra part and a little firmware change, and I can add it if you like).
  • Frosted base. A frosted acrylic or white plastic base lights up softly from the LED.
  • Sparkle inside. For a hollow cube, a few fairy-light beads or glitter in a clear sealed tube next to the screen.
  • Photo of the pair. A small photo frame of the two of you on the back face or the base.

Gift-wrapping ideas

  • A "meet Lumi and Bumi" story card: Lumi lives with you, Bumi with her, and they send each other notes.
  • A picture card for the buttons: "A = Hi 😊 / hold A = Miss you ⭐ / B = Love you ♥ / hold B = Good night 🌙" and "both = quiet".
  • A big "Wi-Fi plug-in" sticker card for her parents with the setup steps and the power cable.

Keep it safe

  • No small loose parts: stick ears, caps and stickers down firmly.
  • Use acrylic instead of glass for a young child, and round or tape any sharp edge.
  • Glue with hot glue carefully or use double-sided foam tape. Don't leave the electronics exposed.
  • Keep the cable short and tucked away. Use a proper USB-C charger from a trusted brand.

Layout inside any housing

PartSuggestion
OLED2 mm standoffs or foam tape. Keep the screen as close to the glass or window as possible.
ESP32-C3USB-C port facing the cable notch or slot. Leave 5 mm of free space around the antenna end, and keep it away from metal.
BuzzerClose to a hole or gap. Sound is muffled in sealed glass. A few 2 mm holes in the base work well.
Buttons A and BFront edge or the side of the base so the screen isn't blocked. Make A and B feel different, for example one has a sticker or a different cap colour.
CableAdd a small strain-relief loop or a cable clip inside. Pull the cable through a notch, not a tight hole. Use a 1.5–2 m cable so it reaches an outlet.
WiringSolder the wires or use a small perfboard for the final build. Loose jumper wires will work loose over time. Cover bare joints with heat-shrink or tape.
HeatThe parts barely warm up (a few hundred milliwatts), so no vents are needed, but don't wrap them in foam.
AccessKeep the USB-C port reachable, or leave a lid that opens, so you can update the software later.
Mock it up first. Make a cardboard version with real measurements and tape the parts inside before you cut, print or glue anything. It takes 20 minutes and avoids most mistakes.

Software settings I can add for the housing

Different housings change how the screen sits, so these would be useful options in config.py. Tell me which you want and I'll add them to the firmware:

  • Rotate 180°, if the screen ends up upside down. (Today you'd edit two lines in ssd1306.py.)
  • Mirror left-right / flip top-bottom, needed for the prism option C.
  • Brightness / contrast setting, so the picture can be dimmer in a dark bedroom or bolder behind thick glass.
  • Night mode: the screen dims or turns off between set hours.
  • Buzzer volume option (a quieter duty cycle) for bedside use.
